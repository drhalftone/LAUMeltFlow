Finite volume vs finite element

Boxes and Tents

Two ways to turn a smooth temperature field into a finite list of numbers. Same bar, same physics, completely different bookkeeping.

The problem both methods solve

Heat flows into one face of a slab. The temperature inside varies continuously with depth, meaning it has a value at every one of infinitely many points. A computer cannot store infinitely many numbers, so before anything can be solved, the continuous field has to be replaced by a finite set of unknowns.

That replacement step is called discretization, and it is the only thing separating the two methods below. They disagree about what the finite numbers should mean.

1Finite volume: boxes and fluxes

Cut the slab into boxes. Each box stores one number, understood as the average temperature over that box. Not a value at a point, an average over a region.

Then ask a bookkeeping question about each box: how much heat crossed each of its two faces? Here the heated surface is on the right, so heat travels leftward into the material. A box takes heat in at its right face and passes it out at its left. The stored energy changes by the difference, plus anything generated inside.

energy change in box i = (heat in at right face) − (heat out at left face) + (heat made inside)

No derivative was ever written down. The method is a balance sheet.

A real solver does not hardcode a direction. Each face flux is computed from the temperature difference across it, so the sign takes care of itself and the same formula works whichever way the heat happens to be going.

This is your solver. Each finite-volume cell holds an average temperature and a set of species densities, and each step balances the fluxes across its two faces.

2Finite element: tents and fitting

Start somewhere else entirely. Instead of averaging over regions, assume a shape for the answer and then find the best version of that shape.

Place nodes along the bar. At each node, define a simple tent function: it rises to one at its own node and falls to zero at the neighboring nodes, and it is zero everywhere else. Scale each tent by an unknown height and add them all together. The sum is a piecewise-linear curve, and the unknowns are the tent heights.

T(x) ≈ T₁·tent₁(x) + T₂·tent₂(x) + T₃·tent₃(x) + …

That guess will not satisfy the governing equation exactly, so there is a leftover error called the residual. Finite element picks the heights by demanding the residual be zero on average against each tent: multiply the residual by a tent function, integrate over the bar, set it to zero. One equation per tent, as many equations as unknowns.

Where finite difference fits

The third method is the one people confuse with finite volume, not with finite element. Finite difference keeps neither boxes nor shapes. It stores values at points and replaces each derivative with a difference quotient taken from a Taylor expansion.

On a uniform one-dimensional mesh, finite difference and finite volume often produce identical algebra. The distinction is in the reasoning, and the reasoning is what buys the guarantee. Finite volume arrives at the formula through a flux balance, so conservation holds no matter how coarse the mesh. Finite difference arrives at the same formula through a truncated series, so conservation is only as good as the truncation.

That is why a solver that must not lose energy over a million time steps is written as a finite volume method, even when the resulting lines of code would look the same either way.
